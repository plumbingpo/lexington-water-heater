Tankless units are clever machines, and that is both their strength and their weak spot. Instead of keeping forty or fifty gallons hot around the clock, a tankless heater springs into action only when water starts moving: a flow sensor detects demand, the control board fires the burner or elements, and water is heated on its way through the heat exchanger. Every step is watched by a sensor, and if any reading drifts outside its expected range, the board shuts the heater off and displays a code. For Lexington, KY homeowners, that usually traces back to a short list of causes: scale building up inside the heat exchanger, a sticking or dirty flow sensor, an ignition problem at the igniter or flame rod, or a venting or gas supply issue the unit reads as unsafe.

Error codes are a starting point, not a verdict, and we treat them that way on every Lexington call. The same ignition failure code can come from a worn igniter, a fouled flame rod, a gas line too small to feed the burner at full fire, or an air intake clogged with lint. That is why Lexington Water Heater tests before replacing anything. Our technicians read the fault history stored in the control board, watch flow sensor output with a faucet open, measure flame signal and igniter resistance, and put a manometer on the gas inlet to see what pressure actually reaches the valve when the burner is running flat out. We also compare inlet and outlet temperatures against the flow rate, which exposes a scaled heat exchanger long before it fails outright.

Once the cause is confirmed, most repairs are finished in a single visit. We descale heat exchangers with a pump and descaling solution circulated through the service valves, replace worn flow sensors, igniters, flame rods, thermistors, and combustion fans, clean inlet filter screens, and correct venting problems that trip pressure switches. When the real issue is a gas line sized for the old tank heater, we tell you plainly and quote the correct pipe run, because no amount of part swapping will fix a unit that is starved for fuel. If you are fighting a cold-water sandwich, we explain whether it is normal behavior for your model or a sign of a setting, fixture, or recirculation problem we can correct. Symptoms That Mean It's Time to Call

A heater that is starting to struggle rarely hides it for long, and the clues are easy to spot once you know them. These are the red flags our technicians see most often before a breakdown:

  • Hot water that fluctuates even though nobody touched the faucet
  • The unit clicking or trying to light several times before giving up
  • Leaks under the unit or around its water connections
  • An error code flashing on the unit's display
  • Unusual noises from the cabinet, like a whine, a roar, or a rattle
  • No hot water at a low-flow faucet even though the shower works fine
  • Hot water that cannot keep up with more than one fixture
  • Longer bursts of cold water between hot draws than you used to get

Why Professional Tankless Water Heater Repair Matters

Consider the risks first. A gas-fired tankless heater can burn nearly 200,000 BTU per hour at full fire, and its vent is the only thing carrying carbon monoxide out of the house; a joint that pulls loose or an intake that gets blocked changes that in a hurry. On the electric side, the loads are large, frequently more than 100 amps spread over several 240-volt circuits. Then there is the diagnostic puzzle. Error codes point to a system, not a part, so a flow sensor fault, a scaled heat exchanger, and a bad thermistor can look nearly identical on the display, and guessing gets expensive.

A trained technician with the right instruments gets to the answer faster. Ours read the fault history, measure flame signal and igniter resistance, test flow sensor output, and check gas pressure under full load with a manometer, which is the only reliable way to catch a gas line that holds pressure at idle but sags when the burner ramps up. We descale with a circulating pump and the right solution, then flush thoroughly so nothing corrosive is left in your plumbing. Just as important, you get an honest opinion: if the heat exchanger is damaged on an older unit, we will say so before you spend money on a repair.

Prevention Tips That Actually Help

Routine maintenance is almost always cheaper and easier than an early replacement. Here's where to begin:

  • Keep the vent termination and air intake clear of leaves, nests, snow, and lint
  • Check the inlet filter screen periodically, since a clogged one starves the unit of flow
  • If scale comes back quickly, add a softener or a scale-prevention cartridge on the cold inlet
  • Leave the power on in cold weather, since freeze protection only runs when the unit has electricity
  • Schedule a descaling flush every year; hard water may call for one every six months
  • Treat a code that returns after a reset as a symptom worth diagnosing, not ignoring
  • Have the gas line evaluated whenever you add a range, dryer, or fireplace
