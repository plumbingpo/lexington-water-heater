Keeping Your Paris Water Heater Running Longer

Most premature tank failures start with maintenance that never happened, and the habits that prevent them are easy. Flush the tank every year to clear sediment, get the anode rod checked every few years, and operate the relief valve each year so it doesn't seize. Keep the temperature near 120 degrees to reduce scald risk and slow scale buildup. For a tankless heater in a hard-water area, plan on descaling once a year to protect the heat exchanger.

Take a look at the age of your water heater. The rating plate on the tank usually includes the month and year it was built, and that date says a lot about what comes next. Most tank models reach 8 to 12 years, heat pump units 10 to 15, and regularly descaled tankless units around 20. If your heater is past that range and also shows discolored water, rumbling, or moisture at the base, it's usually due for a planned replacement, and that's almost always cheaper and easier on everyone than a rushed emergency install once the tank fails.

If warning signs do show up, acting quickly keeps them small. A dripping relief valve might point to high water pressure or thermal expansion with nowhere to go, both modest fixes. The same drip left alone for a season can wear out the valve and stress the tank until the tank itself fails, and a small repair becomes a full replacement plus water damage. It's smart to keep our number saved on your phone so you can reach us at the first sign of trouble. Early intervention saves time, money, and cold showers.

Common Water Heater Problems in Paris

Around Paris, water heater trouble tends to trace back to a handful of causes. Depending on the neighborhood and the water source, minerals can be a real factor: calcium and magnesium settle to the bottom of a tank as sediment, forcing the burner or lower element to work harder, which produces the rumbling and popping you may hear during a heating cycle. In tankless units, the same minerals build scale inside the heat exchanger, and that's why annual descaling matters so much in hard-water areas.

Equipment age plays a big role too. When the protective anode rod is gone, often well before the tank itself looks old, the tank begins corroding from within, and a drip from the bottom seam often shows up next. Homes here also run on a mix of fuel types, gas tanks in some houses and electric tanks in others, so our technicians handle everything from worn thermocouples and sooty burners to burned-out elements and failed thermostats.

Code is another piece. Swapping out an older heater frequently means updating the installation along the way: a thermal expansion tank if the water system is closed, a relief valve drain line piped to an approved spot, venting that drafts properly on gas units, and a drain pan or seismic straps where required. Homes on well water have their own issues, such as heavier sediment, iron staining, and the sulfur odor that sometimes shows up when certain water chemistry reacts with a standard anode rod. Every one of these problems is fixable, and most are much cheaper when caught early.
